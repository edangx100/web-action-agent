A beginner's tour of web-action-agent

Teaching an AI to Browse

An AI that answers questions by clicking through real websites, is marked by code instead of trusted, and learns shortcuts from the runs that worked.

You already know how to find something on a website. You look at the page, pick a link, look again, and keep going until the answer is in front of you. A chatbot can't do this on its own. It can read and write words, but it has no mouse, and no reliable way to say which button it means. This project gives an AI those missing pieces, sends it to five Singapore government websites with twelve real questions, and measures how often it gets them right.

Part 1

One question, one right answer

Here is the question the agent gets in the project's demo, in everyday words:

In Singapore, if you earned $80,000 this year, how much income tax do you pay?

The agent may not answer from memory. It starts on the home page of IRAS, the country's tax office, and must find the answer on the site itself.

Singapore taxes income in slices. The first $20,000 is taxed at 0%, the next $10,000 at 2%, the next $10,000 at 3.5%, and so on. There is a pleasant way to picture this. Draw each slice as a rectangle whose width is the money in that slice and whose height is its tax rate. The tax on a slice is width times rate, which is exactly the rectangle's area.

So the right answer is $3,350. To reach it, the agent has to do what you would do: open the tax menu, follow the right links, and read the right row of a table. The rest of this page is about how each of those small acts is made reliable.

Part 2

An AI can read, but it can't point

A language model reads text and writes text. Show it a screenshot and it can describe the page well. Ask it what to click, and it answers in words: “the round button near the top.” Something then has to turn that sentence back into one exact element on the page. That matching is a guess. When it guesses wrong, nothing crashes. The agent clicks the wrong thing and carries on.

The fix is an idea from published research called Set-of-Marks. Before the AI looks at the page, the agent draws a small number on everything a person could click, and gives the AI the same numbers as a list. Now the AI doesn't have to describe anything. It points: “click 4.” The 4 is a position in a list the agent is already holding, so there is nothing left to guess.

[1] link      "Home"
[2] link      "About"
[3] link      "News"
[4] link      "Taxes"
[5] searchbox "Search"
[6] button    "Go"
[7] link      "Individual Income Tax"
[8] link      "Tax residency & rates"

One promise holds this together: the 4 painted on the picture must be the 4 in the list. If those ever disagreed, every click would be a coin toss, and the logs would blame the AI's reasoning. The code keeps them in step by working out where every number goes as plain arithmetic on rectangles first, and painting the picture only after.

The page can lie

Building that list sounds easy. The trouble is that a web page will happily report things a person can't actually click. Picture a page with a cookie dialog. Underneath it, every link still has a real position and a real size. Code that only reads the page would offer all of them, and the AI would click one after another, puzzled that nothing happens.

So the agent asks the browser four questions about every candidate. Can a person operate it? Is it switched on? Is any of it on screen? And would a click aimed at it actually land on it? The last one is answered by asking the browser what sits at five points inside the element. If something else is on top at those points, the element is covered.

The AI gets the numbered list and the page's main text on every step. The screenshot with numbers drawn on it is sent only when the text looks too thin: fewer than five clickable things found, or two failures in a row. Pictures go to a slower, pricier model that can see, and most steps don't need one.

Part 3

The loop, one step at a time

With a way to see and a way to point, the agent works like a patient person. Each step goes round the same loop: look at the page, let the AI decide one action, check that action, and act on it in a real browser. The run ends when the AI chooses to finish, or after 25 steps at most.

Press Next step to follow the real demo run on the IRAS website, one stage at a time.

Ready

The agent opens a fresh, private browser on the IRAS home page. It has the question and nothing else.

$ waa run --start-url https://www.iras.gov.sg/ "…"

Decide: seven moves, nothing else

The AI can't reply with any sentence it likes. Its answer must be exactly one of seven actions, in a fixed format that the AI service enforces while the reply is being written:

  • click
  • fill
  • press
  • scroll
  • go to
  • use a skill
  • finish

There is no eighth action, so the AI has no way to run code or do anything nobody planned for. There is also no loose sentence for the program to misread.

An AI also has no memory between calls. Each step, the agent writes a fresh note of what has happened so far. The last five steps go in word for word. Older steps are folded into a short tally, and the tally is made by counting, not by asking another AI to summarise. A price like “$12.99” can never drift into “about $13”. Thirty steps of memory take 1,717 characters; three thousand steps take 1,741.

Check: a gate in front of the browser

A well-formed action can still be a bad one. click 12 is perfectly formatted on a page with eight numbered things. So every action passes a gate before the browser sees it. Is it one of the seven actions? Does the element exist on the page exactly as the AI saw it? Is the address one this run is allowed to visit?

The important part is what happens on a no. Nothing crashes. The AI gets back a plain sentence saying what was wrong and is asked again, up to two more times. A specific sentence, naming the number that doesn't exist and the numbers that do, usually fixes the next try. Try a few proposals on the page from Part 2:

allowed

Element 4 is the link “Taxes” on this page, and the click keeps the run on iras.gov.sg. The browser does it.

If three proposals in a row are refused, the run stops with a stated reason instead of quietly doing nothing. The agent is also a polite visitor: it waits at least a second between visits to a site, names itself honestly in every request instead of pretending to be a person's browser, and obeys the site's robots.txt, the file where a site lists what automated visitors should leave alone.

Part 4

Marked, not trusted

When the agent stops, it says “success” and gives an answer. On its own, that claim counts for nothing. Every task in the project's benchmark comes with checks that a program runs on the finished run. Is the answer right, written any of the usual ways? Did the run end on the right page?

Why check the page as well as the answer? In one task, on the Ministry of Education's school finder, the agent named the correct school without ever applying the filters the task asked for. It had answered from what the AI already knew. The page check failed it. An agent that guesses well on easy questions will guess wrong on the ones that matter, so the tasks check how it got there.

Who checks the checker?

Some answers can't be checked by code. The latest population figure, for example, changes every year. Those go to a second AI acting as a judge, from a different company than the agent's model; the program refuses to start if the two match. The judge also names the cause of every failure.

Then a person checked the machine's marks by hand on 20 runs, seeing the evidence first and the machine's verdict last.

Part 5

Learning shortcuts

Every step of working something out costs an AI call, and AI calls are most of the money and most of the waiting. But questions repeat. Once the agent has found its way to the tax rates page, why work the route out again?

So a run that passed can be turned into a skill: the same route, saved as a small recipe with blanks where the task's details were. The next time a task starts on the same site, the AI is offered the skill. Choosing it costs one AI call, and the browser then replays every saved step on its own, with no AI involved at all.

Saving a click so it still works next month

There is a catch. The numbers on the page are thrown away and redrawn on every single look, so a recipe that says click 4 is like a shopping list that says “the third thing on the left”. It breaks as soon as anything moves. So the skill saves descriptions of each element, three of them, tried in order:

A recipe learned from one run has only proved that it can repeat itself. So before a skill is kept, it is replayed on the live site with different values, and it has to work. Only then does it go into the skill library. Each skill is a small text file a person can open, read, disagree with, and delete.

Part 6

Did it work?

The project's benchmark is 12 read-only questions on 5 Singapore government websites: the Ministry of Manpower, the Ministry of Education, IRAS, the statistics office SingStat, and the CPF Board, which runs the national savings scheme. Every question was run on the live sites in October 2026, two or three times each. The AI behind the agent is NVIDIA's open Nemotron 3 Nano model unless stated otherwise.

Do learned skills help? Yes.

The benchmark ran twice on the same model: cold, with no skills, then warm, with the three skills learned from the cold run's passes.

Read the split, not the headline. The nine questions with no skill ran exactly as before, yet still rose from 6 to 10 passes. That rise is chance, and it shows how much a small benchmark wobbles from one run to the next. The clean result is the middle pair.

Is it tied to one AI model? No.

The same benchmark, cold, on three similar-sized open models from three makers, changing nothing but one setting:

The same six questions defeated all three models, so those failures belong to the agent and the websites, not to any one model. The project also sent one task through three very different AI services, a marketplace, NVIDIA's own service, and a server it ran itself on a rented GPU, without changing a line of code. All three passed.

Do screenshots help? Barely.

Reading pages as text alone passed 10 of 24 trials. Adding screenshots when the text looked thin passed 11 of 24, for 10% more cost. One extra pass is within the noise. On these sites, the agent doesn't fail for lack of seeing the page.

Part 7

Why it fails

Across all 195 marked trials, 81 passed and 114 failed. The judge gave every failure a cause, which turns a failure rate into a list of things to fix.

Planning
the wrong idea: gave up early, skipped a filter, scrolled in circles, guessed
Navigation
lost between pages, or guessed web addresses that didn't exist
Grounding
the right idea, the wrong thing on the page

The right idea, the wrong box

On the Ministry of Education's school finder, the task was to filter the list to primary schools in the Bishan area and report how many there are. The answer is 3. The agent knew it had to filter by area, but it typed “Bishan” into box [4], a box for finding schools near a home address, instead of choosing Bishan from the dropdown [5].

Stuck behind a pop-up

On the Ministry of Manpower site, the task was to find how many days of annual leave an employee gets in their fourth year (the answer is 10). On a first visit, the site shows a “take a short tour” box. Behind the box, a see-through dark shade is laid over the whole page, so a click aimed at any link lands on the shade instead. The way out is one click on [5] Skip.

The two Ministry of Manpower tasks failed all 32 of their trials, on every model. Teaching the agent to dismiss a pop-up before anything else is the clearest next improvement, and now there is a measurement to prove whether it worked.

Part 8

What to take away

Strip away the details and four ideas remain. Each one applies to almost any AI that acts in the world, not only to this one.

  1. Let the AI point instead of describe.Numbers on the page turn a vague sentence into an exact choice, and remove a whole class of silent mistakes.
  2. Check every action before it happens, and explain every refusal.A closed list of seven moves and a gate in front of the browser keep the AI inside safe bounds. A clear reason gives it something to fix.
  3. Mark the work with code, then check the marker against a person.An AI saying “success” is a claim. A program checking the answer and the page is evidence.
  4. Learn from success, but prove the lesson first.A skill is kept only after it works again with different values. Then it does the same job in a fifth of the steps.

The limits are stated plainly too. Two or three trials per question is a small sample, so differences of a trial or two are noise. The agent only reads public pages: no logins, no forms that change anything, no purchases. Some websites refuse a browser with no visible window, and those were left out of the benchmark.

Everything above can be re-checked without an account or a key. The project ships with about 1,600 tests and a small practice website that runs on your own computer, and a single command re-runs the whole evaluation offline on it.

The code, the results tables and one complete recorded run are at github.com/edangx100/web-action-agent.
